Where this sits Unit 2's writing task is a biographical account, and its language focus explicitly pairs "combining techniques" with "spelling strategies." This session tackles both together: building a short, vivid biographical character sketch while practising a concrete method for locking in tricky spellings — rather than treating spelling as a separate, disconnected drill.

Learning objective

By the end of this session, the student will be able to:

  • Combine at least two descriptive techniques — simile plus a non-visual sensory detail (sound, smell, touch) — in a single description of a real person.
  • Apply a specific spelling strategy (chunking a word into parts, or a memory trick) to four commonly misspelled words relevant to this unit.
  • Write a short biographical paragraph about someone he knows that uses both techniques on purpose.

Why this matters for the exam

Markers reward writing where techniques work together rather than description that's just "one simile, then stop." A sentence combining a simile with a sensory detail ("his laugh rolled out like thunder, and he smelled of engine oil and peppermint") shows more control than either technique used alone. Spelling accuracy on commonly-tested words is also a flat, easy mark to secure — a five-minute strategy now pays off in every piece of writing this year.

A note on the text used

This session uses an original short character sketch, "My Grandfather's Hands," written to demonstrate simile and non-visual sensory detail working together, on the same intergenerational theme as 'Grandmother's Song'. Swap in the real text once the Workbook 8 arrives — the technique-spotting and the "write about someone you know" task both transfer directly.

Likely confusion point Students often default to only visual description ("she had brown hair, blue eyes") because it's the easiest to picture. Explicitly banning sight in the warm-up forces a shift to smell, sound and touch, which tends to produce far more original, memorable writing.
Likely confusion point A simile that isn't actually connected to the person's character can feel bolted-on ("her hair was like a golden waterfall" tells us nothing about who she is). Push for similes that reveal personality, not just appearance — see the script for how to redirect this.
