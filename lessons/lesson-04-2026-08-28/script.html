Session 4 · Friday 28 August 2026

Teaching Script

Exactly what to say, plus how to push a flat simile into one that actually reveals character.

📋 Lesson plan 📝 Homework worksheet 🖥️ Session slides 🗣️ Teaching script

Before you start

Have the slides ready. No extra materials needed today — this session runs almost entirely on discussion and his own memories of a real person, which tends to make it one of the more enjoyable sessions of the four.

The one idea this whole lesson hangs on A description is only doing real work if it tells you something about who a person is, not just what they look like. Every time he offers a description today, your job is to gently ask: "And what does that tell us about them, as a person?" That single follow-up question is the engine of the whole lesson.

0–3 minWarm-up: describe someone without sight

Say this
"Think of someone you know really well. Now describe them to me in one sentence — but here's the catch, you're not allowed to mention how they look. No hair, no eyes, no height. Nothing visual."

Give him a moment to think — this is genuinely a bit hard on the spot, so don't rush it. If he's stuck after 20–30 seconds, prompt with: "What does their voice sound like? What do they smell like when you hug them? What does their handshake or hug feel like?" Whatever he comes up with, respond with real curiosity before moving on — this is setting up the whole lesson's mindset.

3–10 minTeach: simile with purpose, then sensory detail

Move to slide 3.

Say this
"You already know what a simile is — comparing one thing to another using 'like' or 'as'. But here's the upgrade: a weak simile just describes looks. 'Her hair was like a golden waterfall' — okay, but so what? It doesn't tell us anything about who she actually is. A strong simile reveals character. 'Her laugh was like a door swinging open' — suddenly we know she's warm, welcoming, maybe a bit loud and sudden. Same technique, completely different power."

Move to slide 4.

Say this
"The second tool is sensory detail that isn't about sight — because sight is the one everyone reaches for first, and it's actually the least memorable. Sound: how someone laughs, what they always say. Smell: what their kitchen, their car, their jacket smells like. Touch: what their handshake or hug feels like. These stick in your memory way longer than 'she had brown eyes.'"

Move to slide 5, showing them combined.

Say this
"And the real skill — the thing that gets top marks — is combining them in the same sentence or two, so the description feels layered rather than like a checklist."

Move to slide 6 for the spelling strategy — keep this section brisk, it's a supporting skill today, not the main event.

Say this
"Quick five-minute detour — four words that trip almost everyone up, and a trick for each. 'Definitely' has the word 'finite' hiding right inside it — de-FINITE-ly — that stops the common mistake of writing 'definately'. 'Necessary' — one collar, two sleeves, meaning one C, two S's. 'Believe' — never believe a lie, so it's i before e. And 'resemble' — think of 'assemble', same ending pattern, re-SEMBLE."

Say each word slowly, exaggerating the "trick" part of it with your voice, and have him repeat each one back once. That's genuinely enough repetition for a first exposure — you'll reinforce it again through Task 4 of the worksheet.

10–20 minGuided analysis: "My Grandfather's Hands"

Move to slide 7, read the whole passage aloud once, warmly, at a natural pace. Then move to slides 8–9 to go through it in two halves.

Say this
"Let's find where this combines simile and sensory detail — and every time we find one, I want us to ask: what does this actually tell us about who this person is?"
LineWhat to draw out
"rougher than the bark of the old tamarind tree"Touch + simile combined in one phrase. Ask: does "rough hands" alone tell a story, or does comparing them to old tree bark add something — a sense of years of outdoor work, of being weathered and strong?
"the sound rolls out of him like thunder arriving late to a storm that has already passed"Sound + simile. This is a longer, more unusual simile — ask what "arriving late" adds. (Possible answer: it suggests his laugh feels inevitable, a bit delayed and rolling, not sharp or sudden.)
"He smells of engine oil and peppermint... it just smells like safety"Smell, and note the writer explicitly names the character insight ("safety") rather than leaving it implied. Ask if he prefers description that states the feeling directly, or leaves it for the reader to work out — there's no wrong answer, it's a genuine stylistic choice worth discussing.
"made of the same stuff as the mountains... solid, unhurried, impossible to move"The big one to slow down on. Ask directly: "Solid, unhurried, impossible to move — what kind of person is that, in terms of personality, not just size?" Push toward answers like: calm, dependable, stubborn in a good way, someone who doesn't panic.
If his answers stay surface-level ("it means he's big") Ask a sharper follow-up: "Would you say the same thing about someone who was just physically large but nervous and anxious?" That usually breaks the literal reading and gets him to separate physical size from the calm, dependable character trait the simile is actually pointing at.

20–27 minGuided practice: his own description

Say this
"Now it's your turn — take the person you thought of at the very start, and write 4 to 6 sentences describing them. I want at least one simile that reveals character, not just looks, and at least two sensory details that aren't about sight."

Let him write with minimal interruption for a couple of minutes. If he reaches for a purely visual simile, gently redirect using the character-reveal question rather than rejecting it outright: "Nice image — now what does that tell us about who they are as a person? Can you tweak it so it shows that too?"

Say this
"Read it back to me. Which sentence do you think is the strongest, and why?"

Asking him to self-select his strongest line (rather than you pointing it out) builds the editing instinct he'll need for independent writing — it's a small habit worth repeating across future sessions.

27–30 minWrap-up and homework

Say this
"Really strong session. I've sent the worksheet — Task 1 is spotting techniques, Task 2 asks you to fix a flat simile, Task 3 is questions on the grandfather passage, Task 4 practises those four spelling words with the cover-write-check method, and Task 5 is your longer version of what we just did together, describing someone you know in 5 to 8 sentences."
Say this
"That's four sessions down — nice work. You've now got tools for controlling tense, poetic form, dialogue punctuation, and rich description, all feeding into what you're doing in class right now with Unit 2. We'll build the next block of lessons from here."

After the session — quick self-check for you

  • Did his final description include a simile that genuinely reveals character, or did it stay visual/surface-level even after prompting? If it's still surface-level, that's worth another round of focused practice in a future session rather than something to push harder on today.
  • Across all four sessions so far, is there a pattern in what needs the most repetition — tense control, technical punctuation, or generating original figurative language? Jot a one-line note for yourself; it'll help shape which skills get extra reinforcement when you plan the next batch of lessons.
