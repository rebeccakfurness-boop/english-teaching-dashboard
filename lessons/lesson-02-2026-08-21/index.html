Where this sits Unit 1 formally ends 21 Aug, and its poetry strand (built around Tu Fu's poems in the Learner's Book) covers exactly this — poetic form and the effect of line breaks and pauses. This session gives it focused, hands-on practice, and closes the unit on a high note before Unit 2 ("Grandmother's Song") begins in class on Monday.

Learning objective

By the end of this session, the student will be able to:

  • Define enjambment (a line that runs on into the next with no grammatical pause) and caesura (a pause in the middle of a line, usually marked by punctuation) in his own words.
  • Find and mark both in a short poem, and explain the effect each has on the reader.
  • Deliberately use at least one of each in a short original poem.

Why this matters for the exam

Cambridge English assessment rewards students who can talk about how a writer creates effect, not just what the poem is about. "The poet uses enjambment here to make the sentence rush forward, mirroring how fast the flood arrived" is a much stronger analytical sentence than "the poem is about a flood." This lesson trains that exact move — form → effect — which transfers directly to unseen poetry questions.

A note on the text used

This session uses an original poem, "Aftermath," written specifically to contain clear, unambiguous examples of both enjambment and caesura on the nature-disaster theme that runs through Unit 1. It is not Tu Fu's poetry from the Learner's Book — once you have that text, it's an excellent, richer poem to revisit this exact skill on, since classical Chinese poetry in translation is often built almost entirely around controlled pauses and line breaks.

Likely confusion point Students often think "enjambment" just means "no punctuation," full stop. Push a little further: it specifically means the grammatical unit (the phrase or clause) is not complete at the line break, so the eye/voice is pulled forward into the next line to finish the thought. A line ending on a comma before a list continues is technically still a form of continuation, but the clean teaching case is: if you could not put a full stop there and have it make sense, and the sentence must continue to the next line, that's likely enjambment.
Likely confusion point Caesura is often confused with "just a comma." Emphasise position: caesura is specifically a pause in the middle of a line (not at the end). A poet chooses where inside the line to place that breath — that placement is the craft.
