Teaching Script

Exactly what to say, why, and what to do if a term doesn't land the first time.

📋 Lesson plan 📝 Homework worksheet 🖥️ Session slides 🗣️ Teaching script

Before you start

Have the slides ready to share, plus a pen and paper. Today you'll be drawing arrows and slash marks directly onto the poem text as you talk through it — again, seeing you mark it up live, live-thinking through your reasoning out loud, teaches the skill far better than a poem that arrives already annotated.

The one idea this whole lesson hangs on Poets don't break lines randomly. Every line break and every mid-line pause is a decision, and behind every decision is a question you can teach him to ask: "What does the reader feel, right here, because of where this line ends (or doesn't)?" If you keep returning to that one question, the whole lesson holds together even if the specific vocabulary (enjambment, caesura) gets fuzzy in the moment.

0–3 minWarm-up: notice your own voice

Say this
"I'm going to read two lines out loud. Just listen to what my voice does." (Read slowly, naturally: "The river climbed the bank and did not stop / until it reached the door.") "Did I pause at the end of the first line, or keep going straight into the second?"

He should notice you kept going — there's no natural pause because the sentence isn't finished ("did not stop" needs "until it reached the door" to complete the thought). That noticing, before you name anything, is the whole foundation of the lesson.

3–10 minTeach: enjambment, end-stopped lines, and caesura

Say this
"What you just noticed has a name: enjambment. It's when a line ends but the sentence doesn't — it runs on into the next line with no pause. Poets use it to pull the reader forward, to create momentum, sometimes even to surprise you, because you don't know yet how the sentence is going to finish."
Say this
"The opposite is an end-stopped line — where the thought finishes exactly at the end of the line, often with a full stop. Listen: 'The sky was clear. Nobody thought to worry.' Notice how complete and settled that feels? That's the effect end-stopped lines create — control, or a sense of finality."

Move to slide 5 for the second technique.

Say this
"Now, a different thing entirely — a caesura. This isn't about the end of the line, it's about a pause in the middle of it. Look: 'We counted days — one, two, three — before...' Those dashes force you to slow right down in the middle of the line, almost like you're holding your breath while you count."
If he mixes up caesura and enjambment Give him this one-line test to run every time: "Is the pause at the very end of the line, or somewhere in the middle?" End of the line, sentence not finished = enjambment. Middle of the line, there's a punctuation mark = caesura. It's genuinely just a location question — strip away everything else if he's stuck.

10–20 minGuided analysis: mark up "Aftermath" together

Move to slide 7, then read the whole poem aloud yourself first, at a natural pace — don't over-perform the pauses, just read it like you mean it. Let him hear it as a poem before you take it apart.

Say this
"Now let's go through it line by line. For each one, I want you to tell me: does this line run on into the next (enjambment), does it stop completely (end-stopped), or is there a pause hiding in the middle of it (caesura)? Sometimes a line can do more than one of these things."

Work through it together. Suggested talking points for the trickier lines:

Line(s)What to point out
"It simply breathes out grey, and settles / across the roofs of houses no one"Enjambment twice in a row — ask him what effect that has when it happens repeatedly (it can create a rolling, unstoppable feeling, which fits ash settling over everything).
"built for this kind of weather. We / counted days"A full stop appears mid-line ("weather. We") — that's a caesura — immediately followed by an enjambment carrying "We" into the next line. Point out that techniques can stack.
"and someone laughed, too loudly, at nothing,"Two caesuras in one line (both commas). Ask: what does all this stopping and starting do to the pace of reading this specific line, about someone laughing nervously? (It makes the line itself sound nervous and broken-up, just like the laugh it describes.)
"Some things return. Some things do not."Short, blunt, end-stopped, with a caesura in the middle. Ask why the poet chose short flat sentences here instead of something more flowing — likely answer: it mirrors how final and simple loss actually feels, no decoration.
If he's struggling to explain "effect," not just spot the technique Give him a sentence frame to fill in, out loud, before writing anything: "This makes me feel ___ because ___." Filling that blank is 90% of what an exam answer on poetic technique actually needs — the technical term is almost a bonus on top.

20–27 minGuided practice: write his own lines

Say this
"Your turn. Pick a natural event — storm, drought, wildfire, whatever you like — and write 4 to 6 lines. I want at least one line that runs on into the next without stopping, and at least one pause hidden in the middle of a line."

Give him a couple of minutes of quiet thinking/writing time — resist the urge to fill the silence. If he's stuck after about a minute, offer a starting line to react to rather than writing it for him, e.g.: "Try starting with 'The fields had waited weeks for rain,' and see where that sentence wants to go next."

Say this
"Read what you've got out loud to me. Where did your voice naturally pause? Is that where you put the punctuation?"

This out-loud check is the fastest way to catch a caesura or enjambment that doesn't actually work as intended — if his voice pauses somewhere the punctuation doesn't support it, that's worth a gentle nudge to add a comma or dash there.

27–30 minWrap-up and homework

Say this
"Great — that's Unit 1 wrapped up. You've now covered both of its big language skills: tenses for effect, and poetic form. I've sent a worksheet — Tasks 1 and 2 are quick spotting practice, Task 3 asks you to mark up 'Aftermath' properly and explain two of your choices, and Task 4 is writing your own poem, a bit longer than what we did just now. Task 5 is a one-line sentence pattern I want you to start using every time you write about a poem in an exam."
Say this
"From Monday, your class moves into Unit 2, 'Grandmother's Song' — a short story with a lot of dialogue in it. Next time we meet, on Tuesday, we're going to look at how to punctuate dialogue properly, and how to use punctuation for effect the way we just used it for poetry."

After the session — quick self-check for you

  • Could he independently generate one clean example of each technique, or did he need the sentence frame ("This makes me feel ___ because ___") to get there? Either is fine at this stage — just note it so you know how much scaffolding to offer next time this skill comes up.
  • If time allowed, did he notice the "stacking" effect (a caesura and an enjambment in the same couple of lines)? That's a slightly more advanced observation — a nice one to praise specifically if it came up, since it shows he's starting to read technique combinations rather than spotting them one at a time.
