Session 3 · Tuesday 25 August 2026

Teaching Script

Exactly what to say, in what order, with a physical trick for the one rule that always trips students up.

📋 Lesson plan 📝 Homework worksheet 🖥️ Session slides 🗣️ Teaching script

Before you start

Have the slides ready to share, and something to write with. Today's key visual is very simple — you'll write the phrase "Stop," she said. by hand and literally circle the quotation marks like a little house, with the comma living inside it. That one small drawing fixes the most common punctuation error at this level faster than any explanation of "rules."

The one idea this whole lesson hangs on Dialogue punctuation has two completely separate layers, and naming that separation up front will save a lot of confusion: layer one is the mechanical rules (where the comma goes, when to start a new line) — these are just correct or incorrect, like spelling. Layer two is a creative choice (short sentence vs. question, dash vs. ellipsis) — these are about the effect the writer wants. Keep telling him which layer you're on as you go.

0–3 minWarm-up: same word, three characters

Say this
"I'm going to say the same word three times, and I want you to tell me what's different about the person saying it each time. 'Stop.' ... 'Stop!' ... 'Stop...'"

Say each with a matching tone — flat and firm, sharp and alarmed, trailing and unsure. He'll likely describe the first as calm/serious, the second as scared/urgent, the third as uncertain/trailing off. Land the point:

Say this
"Exactly — same word, completely different character, and the only thing that changed was punctuation. That's what we're mastering today: getting dialogue punctuation technically correct, and then using it on purpose, the way I just did with my voice."

3–10 minTeach: the four mechanical rules

Move to slide 3. Go through the four rules as a quick, confident list — don't over-explain yet, you'll drill the hard one specifically next.

Say this
"Four rules, and honestly you already know three of them without realising. One: every time a new person starts talking, you start a new line — even if it's a one-word answer. Two: the actual words someone says go inside speech marks. Three: the first word of what they say starts with a capital letter, even in the middle of a sentence. And four — this is the one everyone gets wrong, including adults — the comma or full stop belonging to the speech goes inside the closing speech mark, not outside it."

Move to slide 4 for the specific drill.

Say this
"Watch: 'I already told you,' he said. The comma is snuggled right up inside the speech marks, before you close them. Think of the speech marks like a little house — anything that belongs to what was actually said lives inside the house, including its punctuation. Only if the sentence is a question or exclamation does that change what mark you use, but it still stays inside."

Write "Stop," she said. by hand on your paper as you say this, and draw a small roof/box shape around the quotation marks and the comma together, visually showing the comma is "inside the house." This concrete image is what students remember days later, long after a verbal rule fades.

Move to slide 5 — the second, creative layer.

Say this
"Now the fun layer. Once the punctuation is technically correct, you get to choose which punctuation, and that choice tells the reader about the character. Short, blunt sentences — 'No. Not yet.' — sound firm, in control, maybe even a bit stubborn. Questions sound curious or unsure. A dash — like I'm using right now — signals an interruption or a side-thought. Dots, an ellipsis, mean someone's trailing off, hesitating, not finishing their thought."

10–20 minGuided analysis: "The Last Ripe Mango"

Move to slide 6, read the whole passage aloud once at a natural pace with a bit of character in your voice for grandmother vs. child, then move to slides 7–9 to go through it in three chunks.

Say this
"Let's hunt for two things at once: where are the four mechanical rules being followed, and what does each character's punctuation choice tell us about them?"
MomentWhat to point out
"You want the biggest one," she said.Textbook example of rule four — ask him to point at exactly where the comma sits relative to the speech mark.
Grandmother's short lines: "No," "Not yet."Ask: "If grandmother spoke in long, flowing sentences instead, would she seem as wise or certain?" Usually he'll say no — short and blunt reads as someone who has already made up her mind.
The child's questions: "Can I have it now?"Contrast directly with grandmother — the child questions, grandmother states. Ask what that pattern of question-vs-statement suggests about who has the power in this conversation.
"She laughed — not unkindly — and set the mango back"Ask why the writer used dashes here instead of a separate sentence like "She laughed. It was not an unkind laugh." (Likely answer: the dash keeps it feeling like a quick, natural aside inside one flowing action, rather than stopping the scene to explain.)
If he spots the technique but struggles with "why" Ask him to try reading grandmother's lines as if she were rambling and unsure instead, out loud, in a hesitant voice. Then ask which version — the actual short blunt one, or his rambling one — feels more like someone who's telling a genuine life lesson. Hearing the "wrong" version out loud usually makes the right answer obvious fast.

20–27 minGuided practice: fix it, then write it

Move to slide 10. Show the unpunctuated line and work through it together first, live, before he does the worksheet version independently.

Say this
"Here's a line with zero punctuation: 'where are you going asked grandmother to see my friends I said.' Talk me through how you'd fix it, rule by rule."

Let him attempt the whole thing verbally or by typing before you correct anything. Expected correct version: "Where are you going?" asked grandmother. "To see my friends," I said. If he misses the new-line rule or the comma placement, point back to exactly which of the four numbered rules applies — naming the rule number helps him build a mental checklist he can run through independently later.

Move to slide 11.

Say this
"Now write two or three lines of your own — any two characters with an age gap, your choice. One of them should talk in short, firm sentences. The other should ask questions or trail off."

Give him quiet time to write, then have him read it aloud. Reading aloud is doing double duty here — it catches punctuation errors by ear, and it lets you both hear whether the intended character effect (firm vs. hesitant) actually comes through.

27–30 minWrap-up and homework

Say this
"Nice work — that's the trickiest technical rule in the whole unit basically nailed. I've sent a worksheet: Task 1 is quick error-spotting, Task 2 is punctuating a whole unpunctuated passage like we just did together, Task 3 asks about the mango scene, and Task 4 is writing your own longer dialogue scene. Task 5 is just a proofreading check on yourself — go back and check every single closing speech mark before you call it done."
Say this
"On Friday, we'll build on this — looking at how to combine description techniques, like simile and sensory detail, when you're writing about a person, which is exactly what 'Grandmother's Song' asks you to do later in the unit."

After the session — quick self-check for you

  • Did the comma-inside-the-speech-marks rule click, or is it worth a 30-second refresher at the very start of Friday's session before moving on? This rule usually needs to be seen 3–4 times across different pieces of writing before it's fully automatic — that's completely normal, not a sign anything went wrong today.
  • In his own written dialogue, did the "firm" character actually sound firm, and the "hesitant" one actually sound hesitant? If the character voices blurred together, that's a good thing to flag gently and revisit in a future lesson — not something to fix today.
