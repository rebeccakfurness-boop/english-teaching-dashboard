Teaching Script

Written in full — exactly what to say, why, and what to do if he gets stuck. You don't need any prior teaching experience to run this from the page.

Before you start

Have the slides open and ready to share. Have a pen and paper (or a notes app) visible on your side of the call — you're going to draw a simple timeline by hand, and seeing you draw it live makes it click faster than a pre-made diagram. That's it. You don't need anything else prepared.

The one idea this whole lesson hangs on Don't think of "past simple / past continuous / past perfect" as three grammar rules to memorise. Think of them as three different camera moves a writer can make: a quick cut (past simple), a slow pan across something already happening (past continuous), and a flashback (past perfect). If you keep translating the grammar into "what job is this doing for the reader?", you'll always have the right explanation on hand, even if he asks something you didn't expect.

0–3 minWarm-up: get him talking, not you

Say this
"Before we start on tenses — quick question. Who wins, a person or a mountain? A person or the sea?"

Let him answer however he wants (he'll probably say the mountain/sea, or make a joke — either is fine, the goal is just to get him talking and thinking about nature's power, which is the theme of everything he's reading in Unit 1 right now). Follow up with:

Say this
"Right. So today we're looking at how writers describe something enormous and out of control — like a volcano or a flood — happening to ordinary people. And the secret weapon they use isn't fancy vocabulary, it's actually just which tense they choose. Sounds boring, but it's one of the highest-value tricks you can learn for your writing marks."

That last line matters — naming that this is an exam-relevant, "secret weapon" skill (rather than "grammar we have to do") changes how seriously a 13-year-old listens.

3–10 minTeach: three tenses, three jobs

Move to slide 3 ("Big idea"). Introduce the framing below before you get into any technical terms.

Say this
"Every sentence about the past is doing one of three jobs. Job one: telling you what happened next — bang, bang, bang, one thing after another. Job two: showing you something that was already going on when something else suddenly happened. Job three: telling you something that happened before the story even started — the backstory. Those three jobs have grammar names, but the jobs matter more than the names."

Now go through each one on its own slide (4, 5, 6). For each, do this three-step pattern:

  1. Read the example sentence on the slide aloud.
  2. Ask him what he notices about the verb.
  3. Give the plain-English rule yourself, then the grammar name.

Past simple — slide 4

Say this
"'The ground shook. The lights went out. People ran.' What do you notice? ... Right — short sentences, one action, then straight to the next one. No messing about. This is past simple. It's the tense you already know best — you use it without thinking. Its job in a story is speed: it makes things feel urgent and fast."

Past continuous — slide 5, with the timeline drawing

Say this
"'She was washing dishes when the plates started to rattle.' Notice 'was washing' — that's two words, not one. That's your clue. Let me draw this."

Now actually draw (on paper, holding it up, or on a shared notes app) a horizontal line. Mark a long wavy bit for "washing dishes" continuing along the line, and then a sharp X where "the plates started to rattle" cuts in.

Say this
"See this wavy part? That's the washing — it's already going, stretched out over time. Then — X — something else interrupts it. That's past continuous: 'was/were plus the -ing word.' Its job is to slow the camera down and build a scene, right before something happens. It puts you inside the ordinary moment just before everything changes."
If he says "isn't that just past simple with -ing?" Good instinct to name — say: "Close, but the key is it needs 'was' or 'were' in front. 'She washed' is one complete action. 'She was washing' means it was still happening — unfinished — at that moment." If he's still unsure, give him a second minimal pair on the spot: "I ate dinner" (done, complete) vs "I was eating dinner when you called" (in progress, interrupted). Ask him which one sounds like it's still going on. That usually lands it.

Past perfect — slide 6

Say this
"'For three hundred years, the volcano had slept.' This is the trickiest one, so here's the phrase I want you to remember: past perfect is 'the past of the past.' It's for something that happened even before the story's 'now'."

Extend your timeline drawing: add a second, earlier point further to the left of the line, labelled "volcano had slept (300 years)", with an arrow pointing forward to where the "now" of the story begins.

Say this
"Imagine the story starts today. Past simple and past continuous both happen 'today' on this timeline. But past perfect happens before today — it's backstory, told without stopping to write a whole new flashback scene. The pattern is 'had' plus the past participle — had slept, had planted, had grown up. If you can slot 'had' in front of the verb and it still makes sense, it's probably past perfect."
If the timeline still feels abstract Ground it in something personal: "Think about today. Before you logged on to this call, you'd already had breakfast, right? 'You'd already had breakfast' — that's past perfect, because it happened before the 'now' of this call." Personal, real-life examples land faster than invented ones when a concept is abstract.

10–20 minGuided analysis: hunt through the passage together

Move to slide 7, then 8–10 (the three paragraphs of "The Night the Mountain Woke"). Read the whole passage aloud yourself first, at a natural pace, so he just experiences it as a story before you dissect it.

Say this
"Okay — now we're going to go back through as detectives. I'll read a sentence, and you tell me: past simple, past continuous, or past perfect? Then we'll talk about why the writer chose it."

Work through paragraph by paragraph. Suggested exchanges — you don't need to say these word for word, but use them as a model for the level of explanation to aim for:

SentenceWhat to askWhat a good answer sounds like
"For three hundred years, the volcano had slept.""What tense, and why start the whole piece with it?"Past perfect — it's backstory. Starting here tells us the danger has been building for a long time before anything happens, which makes it feel more shocking when it finally does.
"Then, one warm evening in April, the ground began to hum.""Why switch to past simple right here?"This is the moment the story's 'now' actually starts — the writer is signalling "and now the real event begins."
"She was washing dishes when the plates started to rattle.""What's the interruption?"The plates rattling interrupts the washing — the ordinary moment is broken by the first sign of danger.
"Ash fell like grey snow, and the whole village was moving...""Why go back to past simple/continuous mixed together here at the end?"Short past simple bursts (ash fell) speed things up, while "was moving" stretches out the image of the whole village in motion — it's cinematic, like a wide shot.
If he gets a label wrong Don't just correct and move on — ask "What made you think that?" first. Nine times out of ten his reasoning reveals exactly which of the two confusion points above (continuous-vs-simple, or the 'past of the past' idea) needs one more example. Fix that specific gap, not the whole concept again.

20–27 minGuided practice: rewrite for effect

Move to slide 11. This is where he does the thinking, not you.

Say this
"Here's a plain sentence: 'The waves hit the shore.' First — rewrite it in past continuous. Say it out loud or type it in the chat." (Wait for his answer — likely something like "The waves were hitting the shore.") "Nice. Now — what changed about the feeling of that sentence?"

Guide him toward: past simple feels like one clean, sudden hit; past continuous feels ongoing, relentless, like it's still happening as you read it. If he doesn't get there himself, offer it as a suggestion and ask if he agrees or would put it differently — his own phrasing sticks better than yours.

Say this
"Now — add one past perfect sentence in front of it, giving some backstory. What had happened before the waves hit?"

Accept anything reasonable ("The storm had been building all week," "No one had expected the tide to rise so fast"). The goal isn't a perfect sentence — it's proving he can deploy past perfect on demand, not just recognise it in someone else's writing.

27–30 minWrap-up and homework

Say this
"That's exactly what you're doing for homework — writing the opening of your own disaster scene, using all three tenses on purpose. I've sent you a worksheet: Tasks 1 and 2 are quick warm-ups like what we just did, Task 3 asks a few questions about today's passage, and Task 4 is your own writing. Don't rush Task 4 — plan your one past-perfect backstory line and your two past-continuous moments before you start writing, the same way we just did out loud."
Say this
"On Friday we're switching from prose to poetry — same theme, nature overwhelming people, but we'll look at how poets use line breaks and pauses to control pace instead of tenses. See you then!"

After the session — quick self-check for you

  • Did he label at least 4 of the 6 sentences in the analysis stage correctly, or with reasonable reasoning even if the label was off? If not, flag past continuous vs. past perfect as something to revisit briefly at the start of Friday's session.
  • Did his rewritten "waves" sentences actually change tense correctly, or did he default back to past simple? A tendency to default to past simple under pressure is completely normal at this stage — just something to keep an eye on across the next few lessons.
